## Working identity

`Level 7 Dev Loop` is the provisional working identity for the concept. The name is an owner assertion carried forward for evaluation; it does not establish a category, market position, delivery form, or promise.

## Target users and context

The owner-selected initial audience is solo developers and small software teams. Within that audience, the target user is the accountable technical owner—such as a solo maintainer, solo product founder, or technical lead in a small team—who is responsible for consequential change in a software repository while coding agents participate in the work. Other implementing engineers may operate within authority granted by that owner, but accountability for what changes and what is accepted remains human.

The relevant context begins when intent is incomplete, work can span multiple decisions or interruptions, and the cost of losing problem context, scope, authority, or verification lineage is material. Low-consequence experiments and work already governed adequately by ordinary engineering practice are outside the initial target context. Larger organizations are not the initial audience merely because they contain similar job titles; any later audience expansion requires evidence and a new owner decision.

## Evidenced problem

Coding agents can improve task completion and reduce effort, but faster output does not remove the technical owner's responsibility to decide what problem is being solved, bound what may change, assess whether evidence is sufficient, and recover the state of work after interruption. Public studies report recurring visible breakdowns in how agents read projects, interpret intent, follow rules, bound actions, execute work, and report progress. Other studies show reduced user understanding, divergent perceived and measured productivity, security weaknesses in studied tasks, and evaluation results undermined by weak tests or contamination.

Current hosts and code forges provide meaningful execution and merge controls. Those controls address important parts of the risk, but the research did not establish that they preserve the complete problem, decision, authority, evidence, and outcome context across a consequential change. Accountable owners may therefore have to reconstruct that context from conversations, repository fragments, and tool-specific state while still being asked to approve the result.

The evidence establishes a credible problem hypothesis, not its market size or severity. No material source directly measured cross-interruption context loss, cross-host continuity, or demand for a full lifecycle intervention. The public evidence is also largely about professional or open-source developers in general rather than the owner-selected solo-developer and small-team audience. The frequency, cost, segment demand, and highest-need context remain unvalidated.

## Desired outcome

An accountable technical owner can move from ambiguous engineering intent to an evidence-supported software outcome while retaining meaningful control of scope, authority, uncertainty, and acceptance. At any consequential decision point, the owner can understand what is currently known, what remains unverified, what changed, and why the next decision is warranted without depending on chat history or confidence alone.

The desired result is proportionate assurance with less avoidable context reconstruction, scope reversal, and false confidence. It is not process volume. Any improvement in speed, quality, safety, or cost must be demonstrated in the user's real work rather than assumed from agent capability or artifact production.

## Non-goals and boundaries

This concept does not replace accountable human judgment, guarantee correct or secure software, promise productivity gains, or treat agent use as inherently harmful. It does not make low-risk work follow high-ceremony controls, turn evidence collection into worker surveillance, claim regulatory certification, or assume that one workflow fits every repository.

It does not displace host sandboxes, permissions, source control, continuous integration, code review, security analysis, or release controls that already work. It does not authorize autonomous production mutation or let persisted text confer fresh authority. The initial boundary is repository-responsible software change; broader organizational governance and unrelated agent actions are outside it.

This brief does not settle a feature inventory, functional or nonfunctional specification, architecture, technology or vendor choice, interface, implementation plan, delivery sequence, or solution form. Examples in this brief describe context only and are non-normative.

## Success signals

Success would be visible in the target user's real work as fewer material scope reversals caused by misunderstood intent, less time reconstructing decision state after interruption, fewer acceptance decisions made without current verification, and clearer identification of uncertainty and residual risk. Work should remain small enough to review, and the additional governance burden should be lower than the rework or ambiguity it prevents.

The concept should also show that users can resume and hand off consequential work with consistent understanding, that stale decisions are not mistaken for current authority, and that existing engineering controls are complemented rather than duplicated. These signals require a baseline and comparison; producing more records, prompts, approvals, or agent activity is not itself success.

## Assumptions, uncertainty, and validation needs

The concept assumes that a meaningful subset of solo developers and accountable technical owners in small software teams repeatedly encounters continuity or decision-lineage failures that existing host and forge controls do not solve. The selected audience is an owner-directed strategic bet, not a conclusion established by the public research. The concept also assumes that durable repository context can reduce those failures without becoming stale, burdensome, or misleading, and that common meaning can survive differences among coding-agent environments.

Direct demand is unknown. The highest-need segment, current workaround, willingness to adopt, acceptable ceremony, and incremental value over ordinary source control, continuous integration, host safeguards, and adjacent governance products need validation with representative users. The causal relationship between preserved context and better outcomes also needs validation; survey confidence and artifact counts are inadequate substitutes.

The concept should be reconsidered if target users rarely experience the problem, existing controls solve it at lower cost, added process increases delay or rubber-stamping, repository records decay faster than they help, provider-neutral meaning proves impractical, or measured outcomes do not improve. Material change to the target user, evidenced problem, desired outcome, or boundaries makes this brief stale and requires renewed discovery and owner decision.
